How volunteer labor builds monuments for corporate extraction

§ I. The Megamachine You Volunteer For
the

very morning, thousands of software engineers open their terminals and contribute to what they believe is a commons. They fix bugs in projects they don't own, review pull requests from strangers, and push code into repositories maintained by foundations with names that evoke community and openness. They believe they are participating in a gift economy, a space of voluntary reciprocity outside the logic of capital. By the end of this essay, I hope to show that they are wrong — not about their intentions, but about the structure they inhabit.

Lewis Mumford, writing about the ancient labor machines that built the pyramids, identified a form of organization he called the megamachine: a vast apparatus composed of human parts, coordinated by centralized authority, producing monumental outputs that served the interests of a ruling class while appearing to express collective will. The workers were not always enslaved in the conventional sense. Many were conscripted through religious obligation, social expectation, or the simple absence of alternatives. The megamachine's genius was that it converted voluntary participation into systemic extraction without anyone needing to crack a whip. The coordination itself was the coercion.

Open source software is the megamachine of the digital age. Not because anyone is forced to contribute — no one holds a gun to your head when you open a pull request — but because the infrastructure of participation has been engineered, gradually and without conspiracy, into a monotechnic regime that converts volunteer labor into corporate value with extraordinary efficiency. The Linux Foundation, the Cloud Native Computing Foundation, the Apache Software Foundation: these are not communes. They are organizational structures whose governance, funding, and technical direction are overwhelmingly determined by the same handful of corporations — Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, IBM — that extract the most value from the software produced. The volunteer sits at the terminal and believes she is building a commons. She is a component of a machine whose outputs are privately captured.

Mumford distinguished between polytechnics — diverse, life-oriented, locally adapted technical practices — and monotechnics, which subordinate all technical activity to a single logic of power and efficiency. Open source presents itself as polytechnic: look at the dizzying plurality of projects, languages, frameworks, and communities. But this is the false pluralism of a shopping mall. Ten thousand JavaScript frameworks all governed through the same GitHub pull request workflow, all licensed under the same permissive terms, all optimized for the same CI/CD pipelines, all legible to the same corporate hiring processes — this is not diversity. It is a monoculture with varietal branding. The monotechnic is not in the code. It is in the infrastructure of collaboration itself.

§ II. What the Gift Economy Actually Demands
what

The open source community has long described itself through the language of gift-giving. You contribute code freely; others contribute freely to you; a virtuous cycle of generosity produces software that belongs to everyone. This narrative is seductive and almost entirely wrong.

Marcel Mauss's *The Gift* is routinely invoked to lend anthropological gravitas to this story, but anyone who has actually read Mauss knows that his central argument demolishes the open source gift narrative rather than supporting it. For Mauss, the gift is never free. It is the most binding of social technologies. To give is to create an obligation; to receive is to accept a debt; to reciprocate is to sustain a relationship of mutual entanglement. Gift economies do not produce frictionless abundance. They produce dense, asymmetric, culturally specific webs of social obligation that sustain distinct identities and power structures. The gift is how societies cohere without markets. It is not a market failure; it is a different kind of binding.

Gabriella Coleman and Christopher Kelty have both done serious ethnographic work on free software communities and found something closer to Mauss's picture than the libertarian commons narrative suggests — reputation economies, status hierarchies, intense social obligations within small communities. But their findings actually sharpen the problem rather than resolving it. Whatever gift dynamics exist in open source operate at the *micro* level, between individual contributors and small project communities. At the *macro* level — where the economic value is actually captured — the flow is starkly unidirectional. A maintainer spends years building and supporting a critical library. AWS wraps it in a managed service and generates billions in revenue. There is no reciprocal obligation. There is no binding social relationship. There is not even an acknowledgment that a debt exists. When the maintainer burns out, they are replaced or the project is forked. The gift, in Mauss's terms, has been *refused* — and in a true gift economy, refusal of reciprocity is the gravest possible offense, tantamount to a declaration of war.

This is not a gift economy. It is not a market economy. It is what Mumford would recognize as labor conscription laundered through the language of voluntarism. The conscription operates not through force but through the structural dependency of the entire software ecosystem on a contribution model that systematically externalizes the costs of maintenance onto individuals while concentrating the benefits in corporations. The résumé-driven developer who contributes to Kubernetes to get hired at Google is not giving a gift. She is performing unpaid labor for a future employer through the only channel that employer has made legible. The platform — GitHub, specifically — is the machine that makes this conscription feel like choice.

§ III. The Actually Hard Question: Cosmotechnics or Cosmetics?
the

The corporate extraction critique, while true, is not new. It has been a staple of free software discourse since the OSI split from the FSF in the late 1990s, and Tiziana Terranova was writing about free labor on the internet before most current open source contributors were born. The more dangerous claim — the one that most open source advocates are not equipped to address — comes from Yuk Hui's concept of cosmotechnics.

Hui argues that modernity has imposed a single technics on the world: Western, calculative, universalizing, rooted in a specific metaphysical relationship between the human and the cosmos that has been mistaken for the only possible such relationship. Other civilizations developed different technics — not merely different tools but different *relations between cosmic order and technical practice*. Chinese technics, rooted in the Dao; Indian technics, rooted in dharma; indigenous technics, rooted in relational ontologies of land and kinship. Modernity did not merely outcompete these alternatives. It rendered them invisible by establishing its own technics as "technology" per se — as if the relationship between calculation, efficiency, and mastery were a natural law rather than a cultural achievement.

The question for open source is whether it has become the vehicle for exactly this universalization in the domain of software — and this is where intellectual honesty demands more than gesture. It is easy to point to cooperative software movements in Kerala, or Chinese fork ecosystems like Gitee, or indigenous data sovereignty projects in Aotearoa and Canada, and claim that these represent alternative cosmotechnics suppressed by the GitHub monoculture. But do they? A cooperative in Kerala writing Python, using Git, deploying to Linux servers, and organizing sprints with Jira is not practicing a different epistemology of software. It is practicing the *same* epistemology under different ownership. A Chinese fork ecosystem that mirrors GitHub's pull request model under different political constraints is not a different cosmotechnics. It is the same cosmotechnics with a different flag.

This is the hardest admission the thesis requires: the situation may be *worse* than a simple story of corporate capture. If Hui is right that modernity imposes a single technics, then open source has not merely been co-opted by corporations — it was *always already* an expression of the universalizing Western technics, from its foundational metaphors (the codebase as property that can be freed, the license as legal instrument of liberty, the individual contributor as autonomous rational agent) to its deepest technical infrastructure. Git's directed acyclic graph encodes a specific metaphysics of time: linear, branching, reconcilable through merge. The pull request workflow encodes a specific epistemology of knowledge production: individual proposal, peer review, authoritative merge. The English-language README, the Roman-script variable name, the ASCII-dependent syntax — these are not neutral technical choices. They are the calcified residue of a particular civilization's way of knowing through code.

What would a genuinely different cosmotechnics of software look like? This is where most critiques flinch into vagueness, and I want to resist that flinch while acknowledging the speculative risk. Consider: a version control system modeled not on Git's linear-branching temporality but on something like the Māori concept of *whakapapa*, where the past is in front of you (because you can see it) and the future is behind you (because you cannot), and where code's genealogy is not a tree to be merged but a set of ancestral relationships to be honored. This need not remain a metaphor. It would produce structurally different software: conflict resolution governed not by three-way merge algorithms but by protocols of relational priority; attribution that is genealogical rather than transactional; forking understood not as competitive divergence but as the natural differentiation of descent lines, carrying obligations back to the ancestor codebase. Or consider a contribution model built not on the pull request — which assumes an individual agent proposing a discrete change to a centralized authority — but on something like the West African *palaver* tradition, where technical decisions emerge from extended collective deliberation that is itself a form of social maintenance, not a bottleneck to be optimized away.

These sound strange. That strangeness is diagnostic. The fact that we cannot imagine version control without DAGs or collaboration without pull requests is precisely Hui's point: the universalization of Western technics has made its particular metaphysics feel like the natural shape of computation itself. A genuinely polytechnic open source would feel alien — not just differently governed, but differently *reasoned*.

§ IV. Let the Counterexamples Bite
let

If this thesis is true, it should be falsifiable. If open source were genuinely polytechnic rather than monotechnic, we would expect to see: structurally diverse governance models flourishing at scale (not just surviving in niches); economically independent maintenance ecosystems that do not depend on corporate sponsorship for their continued existence; and technically heterogeneous contribution pathways that embody fundamentally different epistemologies of collaboration. The empirical record is not encouraging. Consolidation has accelerated, not reversed. GitHub's market dominance has only grown since the Microsoft acquisition. Permissive licenses (MIT, Apache 2.0) have continued to displace copyleft licenses (GPL, AGPL), precisely because permissive licenses are more compatible with corporate extraction. The foundations that govern the most critical infrastructure are funded by and accountable to the same corporations that extract the most value.

But counterexamples exist, and they should be allowed to genuinely test the thesis rather than be preemptively neutralized. Codeberg, the nonprofit Gitea-based forge, offers a structurally different hosting model — no corporate sponsors, no venture capital, funded entirely by donations and grants. The Sovereign Tech Fund in Germany represents a genuinely novel funding mechanism: public money for public code, with no corporate intermediary. The resurgence of copyleft licensing, particularly the AGPL and the Server Side Public License, represents an explicit attempt to reimpose reciprocal obligation — to force the gift relationship back toward Maussian symmetry. These are real. They matter. And they are tiny. Codeberg hosts a rounding error of the world's repositories. The Sovereign Tech Fund's budget is less than what Google spends on open source in a quarter. The copyleft resurgence remains a minority position in an ecosystem whose defaults are overwhelmingly permissive.

Do these counterexamples disprove the thesis? They would if they demonstrated polytechnic diversity flourishing at scale. They do not. But they do more than merely *exist* — they constitute a toolkit for practitioners who recognize the megamachine and want to exert counterpressure from within it. The engineering consequence is concrete, even if the revolution is not. First, *license choice is a structural decision, not a default*: the next time you `npm init` or `cargo init`, choosing MIT because it is the path of least resistance is a decision to enable unreciprocated extraction. Choosing AGPL or a copyleft variant is an imperfect but real assertion of Maussian obligation — it forces downstream users into a relationship rather than allowing frictionless taking. Second, *platform choice is governance choice*: hosting your project on GitHub is not neutral; it is a vote for the megamachine's infrastructure. Mirroring to Codeberg or self-hosting a Forgejo instance does not overthrow the system, but it builds the structural diversity that polytechnic practice requires. Third, *dependency auditing is political*: run `npm ls` or `pip list` on your production stack and ask, for each critical dependency, who maintains it, who funds the maintainer, and whether any reciprocal obligation flows back. Where it does not, the Sovereign Tech Fund model suggests a mechanism: advocate within your organization for direct funding of the maintainers whose unpaid labor your revenue depends on, not through a foundation that launders the obligation, but directly, with the acknowledgment of debt that Mauss would recognize as the minimum condition of a gift relationship.

These are small moves. They do not dismantle the megamachine. But Mumford himself observed that polytechnic practice has always survived inside monotechnic regimes — in workshops, in gardens, in the margins — and that its persistence is what makes alternatives imaginable when the regime's contradictions become unsustainable. The counterexamples bite not because they are large but because they are *structurally different*, and structural difference, maintained over time, is the seed of genuine polytechnic diversity.

§ V. The Component's Dilemma
the

Software engineers who contribute to open source are not bad people doing bad things. They are, in Mumford's framework, components of a megamachine who experience their participation as freedom because the machine has been designed to feel like a commons. The pull request is satisfying. The green squares on the contribution graph are motivating. The sense of belonging to a global community of makers is genuine. None of this is fake. The social experience of open source is real, even as the economic structure is extractive — just as, in Mumford's framing of the ancient labor corvées, the worker's sense of shared purpose was real even as the monument served the ruler.

Genuine polytechnic practice — the kind that could sustain what Hui calls multiple cosmotechnics — would require not merely different licenses or different funding models, though these matter, but different *infrastructures of collaboration*: tools that embody non-fungible, culturally situated technical reason rather than the universalizing calculative logic that Git, GitHub, CI/CD, and the entire modern DevOps stack take for granted. This is not a call to abandon these tools, which would be as pointless as it is impractical. It is a call to recognize them as what they are — the technical expression of one civilization's cosmology — and to take seriously the possibility that other expressions are not merely possible but necessary, if we want the plurality that open source has always promised and never delivered.

The megamachine runs smoothly. Its volunteers are content. The pyramids are very impressive. But the question Mumford asked, and that we have not yet answered for software, is simple: impressive *for whom*?
