§01
§ I. The Axioms of Peano
peano.1889

ow, the natural numbers arise not from experience but from the form of thought itself. Peano, following Dedekind's Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?, distilled arithmetic to five propositions — each as spare and hard as flint — from which the entire edifice of number shall be raised stone by stone. Let ℕ denote the set in question, 0 its least element, and S the successor function: the engine of increment, the pulse of counting.

These axioms are not merely rules but definitions: they tell us what the naturals are, not merely how they behave. From them we shall peel back layer after layer of abstraction until the continuous real line stands exposed — wrested, astonishingly, from the act of adding one.

Axiomata // Peano 1889
PA.I 0 ∈ ℕ. Zero is a natural number.
PA.II For every n ∈ ℕ, its successor S(n) ∈ ℕ. The act of adding one is always possible.
PA.III For all n ∈ ℕ, S(n) ≠ 0. Nothing succeeds to zero — it is the origin, uncaused.
PA.IV S is injective: if S(m) = S(n) then m = n. Different numbers have different successors.
PA.V If a set K ⊆ ℕ contains 0 and is closed under S, then K = ℕ. This is induction — the inextinguishable flame that propagates truth upward without end.
REF // Dedekind 1888 The successor function encodes the entire structure of ℕ. Remarkably, PA.V — induction — is the only axiom that cannot be expressed in first-order logic without the full power of second-order quantification over sets.
NOTE // Gödel 1931 Any consistent system strong enough to contain PA.I–V will contain true statements it cannot prove. The axioms are sufficient for number theory; they are not sufficient for truth.
§02
§ II. Integers &amp; Rationals
ℕ→ℤ→ℚ

From these five strokes we build addition and multiplication by structural recursion, then define the integers as equivalence classes of pairs: (a, b) represents the signed number a − b, and two pairs (a,b) ∼ (c,d) when a + d = b + c. The negative numbers are not objects discovered in nature but certificates of subtraction — formal witnesses that a computation would have succeeded had its inputs been larger.

ℤ as a quotient of ℕ × ℕ // Grothendieck group construction Haskell
-- ℤ as equivalence classes of pairs (a, b) ≅ a - b
type IntPair = (Nat, Nat)
equiv :: IntPair -> IntPair -> Bool
equiv (a, b) (c, d) = a + d == b + c
addZ :: IntPair -> IntPair -> IntPair
addZ (a, b) (c, d) = (a + c, b + d)
negZ :: IntPair -> IntPair
negZ (a, b) = (b, a) -- the mirror operator

The rationals follow the same pattern: represent p/q as a pair (p, q) with q ≠ 0, and declare (p,q) ∼ (r,s) when ps = qr. Division becomes, like negation before it, a syntactic device — an invented object whose entire justification is that it makes equations solvable.

NOTE // symmetry The move from ℕ to ℤ is a symmetrization — we demand that subtraction be total. The move from ℤ to ℚ is a rationalization — we demand division be total. Both are quotient constructions: we forget details to gain closure.
REF // Grothendieck The same construction — pairs modulo an equivalence — generates the K-theory groups that classify vector bundles. Peano's arithmetic and algebraic topology share a common engine.
§03
§ III. The Leap into Continuity
ℚ→ℝ.cut

Here the construction arrives at its most vertiginous step. The rationals are dense — between any two there lies a third — yet they are riddled with gaps. The square root of two is missing. So is π. So is every transcendental, of which there are, in a precise sense, incomparably more than the rationals they elude.

Dedekind's answer is the cut: a partition of ℚ into two nonempty sets (A, B) such that every element of A is less than every element of B, and A has no greatest member. Each such cut is a real number — not a name for one, but the thing itself, reconceived as a division of what we already know. The gap in the rationals does not point to a missing object; it constitutes one.

a Dedekind cut in ℚ // √2 without naming it Lean 4
-- A real number as a downward-closed cut in ℚ
structure Cut where
lower : ℚ → Prop
nonempty : ∃ q, lower q
bounded : ∃ q, ¬lower q
downward : p ≤ q → lower q → lower p
open_up : lower q → ∃ r > q, lower r
-- √2, defined without naming its value
def sqrt2 : Cut := {
lower := fun q => q < 0 ∨ q*q < 2
-- proofs of nonempty, bounded, downward, open_up
-- follow from ordered field properties of ℚ
}

Cantor's equivalent formulation uses Cauchy sequences: a real is an equivalence class of rational sequences (q₁, q₂, q₃, …) whose terms grow arbitrarily close, two sequences identified when their difference converges to zero. Where Dedekind sees a real as a place, Cantor sees it as a process of approach — two portraits of the same unseen face.

REF // 1872 Cantor and Dedekind arrived at equivalent constructions independently in the same year — a remarkable unison at the threshold of modern analysis.
NOTE // cardinality The reals are uncountable (Cantor's diagonal argument, 1891). There are strictly more real numbers than there are natural numbers, integers, or rationals — all three of which are equinumerous.
NOTE // gaps ℚ is dense but not complete. ℝ is both. Completeness — every Cauchy sequence converges — is the single property that makes calculus possible.

§ IV. The Table of Ascent
index
Construction chain // Peano → Continuum
Structure Construction Gain Defect repaired
ℕ Peano axioms; successor S Counting, addition, induction — (origin)
ℤ Pairs (a,b) mod a+d = b+c Subtraction total Subtraction may fail in ℕ
ℚ Pairs (p,q) mod ps = qr Division by nonzero total Division may fail in ℤ
ℝ Dedekind cuts or Cauchy classes Completeness: all limits exist Cauchy sequences may not converge in ℚ

At each step a defect in the previous structure is repaired by a quotient construction — an act of deliberate forgetting, in which pairs of objects are declared identical so that an operation which was previously partial becomes total. The integers forget the individual values of a and b, remembering only their difference. The reals forget the particulars of a Cauchy sequence, remembering only its limit.

What is remarkable is that the real line — the substrate of all geometry, analysis, and physics — contains nothing that was not already, in embryo, implicit in the bare act of counting. The continuum is the shadow cast by ℕ, stretched through three quotient constructions into its full, unfathomable length. Peano gave us five axioms; Dedekind showed us they were enough.

NOTE // pattern Each construction is a Grothendieck completion: given a monoid with a partial operation, freely adjoin inverses. ℤ completes (ℕ, +). ℚ completes (ℤ\{0}, ×). ℝ completes (ℚ, Cauchy).
NOTE // beyond ℝ The construction does not end here. ℂ adjoins √-1. The p-adic numbers ℚₚ complete ℚ under a different metric. The surreals extend ℝ to include infinitesimals. Each is a different answer to the question: what are we willing to forget?
